# Go-to-market and launch playbook

The first job isn't building; it's proving that coordinators feel this pain often enough to **use** and **pay for** a dedicated tool. Every build milestone should come with a customer action.

## 1. Pick a beachhead (narrow first)
"Tour operators, DMCs, agencies and wholesalers" is the long-term market. Start with one segment you can reach and talk to:
- **Recommended starting profile:** small-to-mid group tour operators or DMCs (about 2–30 staff) running group trips with 5 or more suppliers each, coordinated today with email, WhatsApp and spreadsheets.
- Narrow further by geography and destination (for example, UK operators running European group tours). It makes outreach, currency, language and referrals easier. The founder decides this (see decisions.md).
- Avoid at first: large operators with an existing reservation system and an IT team, and solo agents booking individual flights and hotels (too few suppliers per trip to feel the pain).

## 2. Validate before building more (stage 1)
**Goal: 15–20 discovery conversations.** Use "Mom Test" rules: ask about their past behaviour, not your idea.
- "Walk me through the last group trip you coordinated. Where did supplier details live?"
- "Tell me about the last time a deadline or confirmation slipped. What happened? What did it cost?"
- "What do you use today to track deposits and balances? Can you show me?" (Being shown a real spreadsheet is a strong signal.)
- "Who else touches this? Who would decide to buy a tool?"
- "What have you tried to fix this? What did you pay for it?"
Only after that, show the prototype and watch where they get confused.

**Signals that count:** they share their real spreadsheet, they ask when they can use it, they introduce a colleague, or they agree to a paid or committed pilot. Compliments aren't signals.

Log every conversation (date, company, role, pain level 1–5, current tool, quotes, next step) so patterns are visible.

## 3. Landing page and waitlist
- The waitlist must actually capture emails before any outreach links to it. Ask one qualifying question: role, company type, and trips per year.
- Keep claims honest: AI and reminders are "early access concept" or "planned" until they're built.
- Track visitors → sign-ups → booked calls. Every sign-up gets a personal email from the founder within 24 hours, asking for a 20-minute call.
- Lead magnet idea: a free "Supplier deadline tracker" spreadsheet template for group trips. It's useful on its own, ranks for search, and every download is a qualified lead.

## 4. Channels for the first 50 conversations (founder-led)
- **LinkedIn:** a personal message to operations managers, trip coordinators and founders at target companies. Keep it short and specific, and ask for advice, not a sale.
- **Warm intros:** anyone in travel you know, plus each interviewee ("who else should I talk to?").
- **Communities:** travel-trade associations and groups for the chosen region (for example ABTA, AITO, ETOA in the UK and Europe; ASTA in the US), and Facebook/LinkedIn groups for tour operators and DMCs. Check each group's rules before posting.
- **Events:** trade shows such as WTM London and ITB Berlin, for in-person conversations once there's a demo.
- **Content:** short posts on real coordination pain ("the 5 supplier deadlines that sink group trips"), built from interview insights.

## 5. Pilot with design partners (stage 2)
- 2–3 companies that will run a **live trip** in Roveboard.
- The offer: free or heavily discounted for about 3 months in exchange for a 30-minute weekly feedback call and permission to use them as a case study.
- Concierge onboarding: the founder sets up their first trip with them, or for them, from their spreadsheet.
- Success: they keep using it without being chased, and say they'd pay. Ask for a letter of intent or a paid pilot to test willingness to pay.

## 6. Pricing (hypothesis to test, not a decision)
- Charge the travel company a monthly subscription. Suppliers are always free.
- Possible structures: per user (seat), per active trip, or tiers by trip volume. Test in interviews: "If this saved you X hours a week, what would you expect to pay?" Then try anchoring with real numbers.
- Start with a simple plan and an annual discount. Don't compete on being cheapest; compete on deadlines not being missed.

## 7. Competitive landscape (research before positioning)
Tour-operator and DMC software already exists (examples to research: Tourplan, Tourwriter, Travefy, Lemax, TourCMS). Check their current features and prices before making comparisons. Likely positioning: **a lightweight supplier-tracking layer that a small team can adopt in an afternoon**, not a full reservation system. The real competitor is often the spreadsheet plus the inbox.

## 8. Market-ready checklist (before public launch)
- [ ] Waitlist captures emails, and there's a follow-up sequence
- [ ] Demo link works on phone and desktop
- [ ] Privacy policy, terms and cookie notice; GDPR basics if serving the UK or EU
- [ ] Clear disclosure of how documents are processed by AI
- [ ] A custom domain and email (hello@…) owned by the founder
- [ ] Analytics on the landing page (sign-up conversion)
- [ ] 3 or more testimonials or pilot quotes
- [ ] Support channel and response-time promise
- [ ] Data export so customers aren't locked in

## 9. Launch sequence
1. Private pilot (design partners) → 2. waitlist cohort invites in batches of 5–10 → 3. public launch (LinkedIn announcement, community posts, Product Hunt as a bonus, not the plan) → 4. referrals from happy pilots.

## Metrics to watch (weekly)
Conversations held, waitlist sign-ups, calls booked, demo-to-pilot conversion, activated companies (see prd.md), weekly active coordinators, and the reasons people say no.
